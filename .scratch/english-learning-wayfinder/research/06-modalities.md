# Research: modality and repair progression

Scope: evidence relevant to connecting grammar, vocabulary/chunks, listening, speaking, pronunciation and repair from early A1 toward everyday conversation and team calls. This is research input for the decision ticket, not a lesson sequence.

## Findings

1. **Use communicative can-do evidence, not lesson completion.** The Council of Europe’s CEFR spoken-language descriptors explicitly allow imperfect performance: at A1 communication depends on repetition, rephrasing and repair; at A2 short utterances still show pauses, false starts and reformulation; at B1 the learner can keep going with hesitation and repair, initiate/maintain/close familiar conversation, and repeat part of a message to confirm understanding. This supports a progression rubric that tracks task success, independence, repair and comprehensibility together rather than grammatical perfection.
   - [CEFR qualitative aspects of spoken language, Table 3](https://www.coe.int/en/web/common-european-framework-reference-languages/table-3-cefr-3.3-common-reference-levels-qualitative-aspects-of-spoken-language-use)

2. **Repair is a target capability from the beginning.** The CEFR Companion Volume has separate interaction-strategy scales for asking clarification, turn-taking and cooperating, including stock phrases to gain time and keep the turn. Its mediation model treats creating conditions for communication and collaboratively constructing meaning as core language activity. Therefore practice should deliberately log whether the learner notices a breakdown, initiates repair, reformulates/describes, checks understanding and resumes the task—not merely whether the original sentence was correct.
   - [CEFR Companion Volume (official PDF), interaction and mediation descriptors](https://rm.coe.int/cefr-companion-volume-with-new-descriptors-2018/1680787989)
   - [Council of Europe: mediation overview](https://rm.coe.int/cefr-webinar-series-2021-1-background-reading/1680a54fb2)

3. **Progress from constrained to open tasks, but retain a communicative outcome.** In a peer-reviewed comparison of controlled and creative oral tasks, creative decision-making/opinion tasks elicited significantly more negotiation of meaning than controlled tasks. A separate study comparing information-gap and personal-information tasks examined repair negotiation and pushed output. The practical implication is a repeating ladder: model/rehearse a small form or chunk → constrained information gap → semi-controlled personal/problem task → novel decision, explanation or story. Keep both controlled tasks (to isolate a weak form) and open tasks (to test transfer).
   - [Chang, 2019, *System*: creative task engagement and negotiation of meaning](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.system.2018.10.015)
   - [Kasper & Ross, 2013, *Linguistics and Education*: repair negotiation across task types](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.linged.2013.01.003)

4. **Task repetition is useful, but test transfer.** In an experimental study of repeated oral tasks, speech rate gains were largest in the first three performances; pauses and self-repairs changed at different repetition points, with overt self-repairs decreasing only after the fourth performance. Repetition can automate formulation, but the next evidence should use a changed prompt or interlocutor so memorization is not mistaken for mastery.
   - [Lambert, Kormos & Minn, 2017, *Studies in Second Language Acquisition*: task repetition and speech processing](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263116000085)

5. **Listening should expose parsing difficulty deliberately.** A 2025 Cambridge study found unreduced English words were recognized more accurately and quickly than reduced forms by L2 listeners, across native and non-native accents. This supports controlled listening that first establishes the lexical item, then contrasts careful and reduced/connected variants (contractions, weak forms, linking, elision), followed by short natural extracts. Record separate evidence for gist, key detail, word-boundary/segmentation and response to clarification; a transcript-only vocabulary score hides parsing failure.
   - [Verbeke, Mitterer & Simon, 2025, *Bilingualism: Language and Cognition*: phonetic reduction and L2 word recognition](https://doi.org/10.1017/S1366728924000955)

6. **Pronunciation target is intelligibility/comprehensibility, not accent elimination.** The CEFR revised phonological-control scale separates sound articulation and prosodic features. Listener-rating research found comprehensibility related to pronunciation, word stress, fluency, lexis and grammar, whereas accentedness was chiefly linked to pronunciation. A 40-hour course using controlled speech, drama/extemporaneous tasks, recordings, feedback and self-reflection reported increased awareness of features contributing to intelligibility. Thus select pronunciation targets by their effect on being understood (high-value sounds, stress, rhythm, chunk boundaries), and assess with unfamiliar listeners/tasks where feasible.
   - [CEFR phonological competence](https://www.coe.int/en/web/common-european-framework-reference-languages/phonological-competence)
   - [Isaacs & Trofimovich, 2017, *Applied Linguistics*: listener judgments of comprehensibility](https://doi.org/10.1093/applin/amu047)
   - [Galante & Piccardo, 2022, *ELT Journal*: pronunciation toward intelligibility and comprehensibility](https://doi.org/10.1093/elt/ccab060)

7. **Interaction produces the evidence needed for speaking progression.** Synchronous online tutoring research found negotiated turns generated substantial output through rephrasing and elaboration and reported speaking gains in its pre/post design. Negotiation-of-meaning research frames breakdown repair as modified input, modified output and feedback. For this workspace, a speaking attempt should therefore capture the interaction outcome and the learner’s repair moves, not only an isolated monologue.
   - [Yanguas, 2019, *System*: speaking progress and meaning negotiation in synchronous online tutoring](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.system.2019.01.001)
   - [Foster & Ohta, 2005, *Applied Linguistics*: collaborative dialogue and L2 development](https://doi.org/10.1093/applin/ami014)

## Decision implications

- Model each capability as a small evidence vector: comprehension (gist/detail/parsing), production (intelligibility/formulation), interaction (turn-taking/repair/task completion), and transfer (novel prompt). Grammar and vocabulary are means that explain failures and enable the task, not independent graduation gates.
- A next-step rule should preserve one successful capability while stressing one adjacent weakness: e.g. familiar chunks + new information gap; known grammar + reduced listening; known topic + spontaneous explanation. Advance when the learner succeeds on a changed task with manageable support, while recurring failure schedules targeted practice and later delayed re-test.
- Start repair training at A1 with a tiny usable set (repeat, slower, what does X mean, I mean…, can you show me?) and expand it with paraphrase, confirmation, turn-holding and problem explanation. Count independent initiation and successful recovery.
- Introduce natural speech progressively by manipulating one variable at a time (speed, reduction, accent, overlap, number of speakers, topic load). “Understands the transcript” and “can follow the live call” must be separate evidence.
- For future team-call readiness, use multi-speaker simulations with a concrete outcome and unexpected turns; score main idea, action-relevant details, response latency/turn entry, repair, blocker explanation and recovery after missed speech. CEFR levels may label the result, but should not replace this task evidence.

## Limits and research needs

The cited studies are generally small or context-specific and do not establish a universal number of repetitions, exposure minutes, or mastery threshold. They support design principles, not fixed schedules. The workspace still needs a local decision on thresholds, support levels, scoring reliability and how `/teach` artifacts encode these observations. Connected-speech work also does not justify exposing an A1 learner immediately to unmodified team calls; the evidence supports graduated manipulation and repeated transfer checks.
