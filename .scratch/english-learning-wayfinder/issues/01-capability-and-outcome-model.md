## Question

What is the smallest useful capability model for this learner's life, social, work, interview, grammar, listening, speaking, pronunciation, vocabulary, and repair needs, and how should capabilities relate without turning into a CEFR-only scorecard or a technical-English-only track?

## Answer

Capability is the ability to achieve a concrete communicative outcome in a situation, not knowledge of a topic and not a CEFR level. CEFR may remain an external orientation, but it is not the primary progress unit.

Use `context × communicative function × mode × support` as a descriptive selection model:

- Contexts are Life, Social, Work, and Interview. Frontend scenarios are important Work contexts, not a separate capability branch.
- Functions include outcomes such as describing, requesting, clarifying, narrating, explaining cause/effect, discussing trade-offs, disagreeing, and checking understanding.
- Modes include listening, speaking, reading, and writing.
- Support ranges from controlled through constrained and semi-controlled to spontaneous.

This is not a matrix to fill exhaustively and not a curriculum generator. A capability may be strong in one mode/support combination and weak in another; the system must preserve that partial profile rather than mark the whole capability acquired. Grammar, vocabulary, pronunciation, and repair are cross-cutting enablers: normally developed through meaningful capabilities, and trained separately only when evidence shows a bottleneck. Repair is both embedded in every capability and tracked as a cross-cutting ability.

The model must support transfer checks across contexts: a function learned in a frontend task should later be tested in a non-technical situation, and vice versa. Scenarios should become progressively less predictable. Team-call capability must test real-time listening/comprehension, unexpected turns, and recovery, not prepared speech alone.
